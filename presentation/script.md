# Weather Witness: 7-minute presentation script, then 3 minutes of questions

Five speakers, seven slides, about 6 minutes 50 seconds with the video and the live demo.

| Slide | Who | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Title | Manayer | 0:00 – 0:25 |
| 2. The video and the cases | Manayer | 0:25 – 1:40 |
| 3. Kuwait City and London | Bashayer | 1:40 – 2:50 |
| 4. Why, and the land and the ocean | Kawthar | 2:50 – 3:40 |
| 5. What the heat costs | Ahmed | 3:40 – 4:30 |
| 6. The live site | Mohammed and Bashayer | 4:30 – 6:20 |
| 7. The verdict | Manayer, then all five | 6:20 – 6:50 |
| Questions | Manayer and Bashayer; Ahmed for money and the stack | 7:00 – 10:00 |

How the judges are brought in, a different way each time:

| Slide | Way |
|---|---|
| 3 | The one real quiz: hands up |
| 4 | "Don't you wonder…": no answer needed |
| 5 | "Picture this": a small story, no answer needed |
| 6 | A judge picks any city in the world, and guesses first |

Nothing funny during or right after the video. Rehearse once with a timer: there are only about 10 seconds spare.

## Slide 1 · 0:00 – 0:25 · Manayer (centre)

"Good morning. We are five people, and we brought a witness: NASA.

It has watched the weather over your house every single day for 45 years. It wrote everything down. And almost nobody read it.

Our challenge is 'Be an Earth System Trend Detective'. So, let's open the case."

*(Click to slide 2. Stand beside the screen.)*

## Slide 2 · 0:25 – 1:40 · Manayer

*(The video fills the whole screen. Click it. Say nothing until the screen goes black.)*

| Video time | On screen | Manayer |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 – 0:08 | A parked car in summer. A lighter on the dashboard. A flash and a bang. | silent |
| 0:08 – 0:13.5 | Black, 5.5 seconds | **"That was only a lighter. For this mother, the heat took her child."** |
| 0:13.5 – 0:24 | The mother speaks. Nothing else on screen. | silent |
| 0:24 – 0:30 | One screen with the cases and their sources. The video ends here and the screen stays. | silent |

*(Read from the screen, slowly.)*

"Read the screen with me.

489,000 people are killed by heat every year.

1,071 children lost their lives in hot cars in the United States.

1,301 people lost their lives during the Hajj in 2024, in heat of 51.8 degrees.

Different countries, same killer.

And the last line is ours, from NASA: a child in Kuwait City now gets 57 more warm nights a year than one born in 1981.

It is all in NASA's records, for free, in files most people cannot read. So it just sits there."

*(Step back. Bashayer walks in.)*

## Slide 3 · 1:40 – 2:50 · Bashayer

"Quick game. Hands up: which city is heating faster, Kuwait or London?

*(Wait for hands.)* Too easy.

*(Point at the red card.)* Kuwait City. A child born in 1981 had 31 unusually hot days a year. A child born in 2015 has 69. Warm nights went from 25 to 82.

*(Point at the green card.)* London? People there complain at 25 degrees, and they are getting hotter too: hot days went from 35 to 47. Nobody gets a private sky.

The difference is here. *(Point at the stamps.)* The UK's pollution is about 24 percent lower than in 2016. Kuwait's is about 18 percent higher. The UK closed its last coal power station in 2024. Kuwait still makes almost all its electricity from oil and gas, and air conditioning takes about 70 percent of it at the summer peak.

So Kuwait should reach its own goal: 15 percent renewable power by 2030. And the UK should keep going."

## Slide 4 · 2:50 – 3:40 · Kawthar

*(Three points. Count them on your fingers. The three cards are your notes, in the same order. Speak slowly; there is time.)*

"So why? We burn oil, gas and coal, and the Earth is about 1.2 degrees warmer.

Don't you wonder where that heat goes? Three places.

*(Point at the yellow card.)* One: the air. Kuwait City gets 0.43 degrees hotter every ten years.

*(Point at the middle card.)* Two: the land. The soil under Kuwait was 23 percent wet. Now it is 18.

*(Point at the blue card.)* Three: the ocean. It takes about 90 percent of the extra heat. A study found Kuwait Bay warming three times faster than the world.

The air heats, the ground dries, the sea keeps the heat."

## Slide 5 · 3:40 – 4:30 · Ahmed

"Now, money. Picture this: two in the afternoon, July, Kuwait. You are outside, working.

No, you are not. It is against the law: outdoor work stops from 11 to 4, June to August.

When work stops, money stops. In 2023 the world lost 835 billion dollars of income because it was too hot to work, says the Lancet Countdown. By 2030, the International Labour Organization expects 2,400 billion a year.

And at home? *(Point at the yellow card.)* This one is ours, from NASA data: Kuwait City needs 15 percent more cooling than in the 1980s. Your air conditioner works overtime, and it does not even get paid.

Heat is not only weather. Heat is an invoice."

## Slide 6 · 4:30 – 6:20 · Mohammed and Bashayer only

*(Mohammed is at the laptop. Bashayer stands on the other side of the screen. Every click is in bold.)*

Mohammed: "We built Weather Witness. Free, no login, 6,266 cities. Bashayer?"

**(Mohammed switches to the live site. Kuwait City is open, scrolled to the top, so the "What NASA sends us" button shows.)**

**(Mohammed clicks the "What NASA sends us" button under the title.)**

Bashayer: *(Point at the codes that appear.)* "This is what NASA gives you: T2M_MAX, PRECTOTCORR. It looks like a WiFi password. A parent just asks: can my child play outside?

So we turned 16,000 days of weather into two lives. Then, now, and what changed. *(Point at a stamp.)* Every change is tested: a real change gets this stamp. No clear change? We say that too."

**(Mohammed taps a number.)**

Mohammed: "Tap any number, and the whole history opens."

**(Mohammed changes the life to Outdoor worker.)**

Mohammed: "Not a kid? Pick a life: worker, farmer, runner."

**(Mohammed opens "Two futures".)**

Mohammed: "And this part looks ahead."

Bashayer: "We test the past, and only real changes count. We measure the pace. Then we carry the line on, for a child not born yet. *(Point at the columns.)* Now. If it keeps going. If we slow it by half.

Mohammed, born in 2050, please."

**(Mohammed clicks 2050.)**

Bashayer: "Same city, later birthday. A what-if, not a forecast."

**(Mohammed opens "More evidence from NASA" and clicks "The ground".)**

Mohammed: "More NASA evidence: rain from satellites, dust, and the ground."

Bashayer: "Your turn. Any city in the world. Somewhere you think is safe."

**(Mohammed types the judge's city, but does not press Enter yet.)**

Bashayer: "Your guess first: hotter, or the same?"

**(Mohammed presses Enter.)**

Mohammed: "Live from NASA. Three seconds."

*(Bashayer reads the yellow note at the top and says if the guess was right. Mohammed goes back to the slides.)*

*(Manayer clicks to slide 7. The other four line up beside her.)*

## Slide 7 · 6:20 – 6:50 · Manayer, then all five in a line

Manayer: "Members of the jury. Sorry: judges. The verdict.

One: the heat is real. It passed the test, and it has no alibi.

Two: it is not equal. Kuwait gets it worse than London.

Three: it can slow down.

The witness was there all along. Somebody just had to ask.

We started with two children. Same city. Same age."

All five together: **"A different childhood."**

## 7:00 – 10:00 · Questions

Manayer and Bashayer take the questions. Ahmed takes the money and the stack. Answers on the stack and the prediction are in judge-questions.md.

## Likely questions, and who answers

Manayer and Bashayer take the questions. Ahmed takes the money ones.

| Question | Answer | Who |
|---|---|---|
| You said London is not getting better. So what is? | Its country's pollution: the UK's CO₂ is about 24 % lower than in 2016. The weather in London still warms, because the air is shared. | Bashayer |
| Why is Kuwait stamped "Starting" on the site if its CO₂ is still rising? | The stamp counts new rules since 2020 (net zero by 2060, announced in 2022); the sentence under it says pollution has not turned yet. | Bashayer |
| Is the 2040 number a forecast? | No. It is the 45-year trend line continued straight. The site labels it "What-if, not a forecast". | Bashayer |
| Where is your ocean data? | Not on the site yet. The slide uses published research (NASA, and a Kuwait Bay study from 2009). Near a coast our grid square is part land, part sea. It is the next step. | Manayer |
| Did you calculate the dollar figures? | No. They are from the Lancet Countdown 2024 and the International Labour Organization. Ours are the cooling need and the day counts. | Ahmed |
| Is this real measured data? | It is NASA's MERRA-2 model fed by observations, plus GPM satellites for rain. | Manayer |
| Did AI build this? | AI helped write the code; no AI runs in the site, and every number is computed from NASA data in the browser. | Manayer |
